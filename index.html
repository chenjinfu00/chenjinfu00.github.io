Research

Finite-time driving incurs an energy cost, and limited measurements constrain what can be inferred about quantum correlations. These three research directions address those constraints through thermodynamic geometry, quantum dynamics, and many-body symmetry.

Finite-time thermodynamics, optimal control, and fluctuations How do finite-time driving and fluctuations shape the cost of a process?

Shortening the duration of a process typically increases the excess work required to drive it. Thermodynamic length sets a lower bound on this work and can be estimated from finite-time measurements. For shortcuts to isothermality, a geodesic in the space of control parameters gives a minimum-cost protocol. The same geometric approach applies to membrane separation of gas mixtures [1, 2, 3]. When individual strokes form a heat-engine cycle, their energy cost limits the output power of the engine. Shortcuts to isothermality give an attainable power bound for Brownian engines operated in this way. Optimal control also identifies the maximum-power cycle when the available control parameters are constrained [4, 5]. The cost of faster driving also depends on the system. Across a first-order transition, system size can change how excess work scales with the driving rate. In individual realizations, work and heat also fluctuate. Including both energy and momentum transfer in their definitions gives fluctuation theorems that apply when the system and its heat bath move relative to each other [6, 7].

Many-body state preparation and open-system dynamics How can quantum states be prepared faster?

Adiabatic preparation starts from a state that is easy to produce and gradually changes the controls until the system reaches the target state. Driving too quickly can trigger unwanted transitions. Quantum geometry guides the choice of driving protocol, while changing the Hamiltonian without changing the target state can widen the smallest energy gap along the preparation path [8, 9]. Coupling a system to its environment provides another route when the target is a thermal state. The coupling can be designed to speed up the slowest relaxation process while preserving the target equilibrium. The dynamics of a driven open system can also be formulated to describe the fluctuations in the work done by the drive and in the heat exchanged with the environment [10, 11].

Multipartite Bell correlations from limited measurements Can few-body correlations reveal many-body nonlocality?

As the number of measurement parties grows, enumerating the facets of the local polytope becomes impractical. The search for tight Bell inequalities can be formulated as an optimization of the ratio of the quantum value to the classical bound. A nondegenerate local maximum of that ratio gives a facet inequality, providing a way to construct tests without enumerating all facets [12]. The next constraint is how many correlations an experiment can measure. Permutation symmetry allows the inequalities to be built from one- and two-body correlations alone. Semidefinite programming certifies the quantum violations in the large-system limit, making these inequalities suitable for testing multipartite nonlocality using low-order correlations [13].
